Google's August 2026 Spam Update Just Rolled Out — Here's What to Check Right Now

📅 August 18, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ RankSorcery Team
🚨

⚡ TL;DR

  • Google released the August 2026 spam update today (Aug 18) via its search status dashboard and LinkedIn — a "normal" global spam update, rolling out over a few days.
  • It targets spam policy violations (scaled content abuse, cloaking, expired domain abuse, etc.) but explicitly does NOT target link spam or site reputation abuse this time.
  • The timing is messy because Search Console has had a separate logging bug since August 12-13 tanking impression/click numbers in the Performance and Generative AI reports — don't confuse that with an actual ranking hit.
  • If you got hit, Google says recovery can take "many months" and only happens after their systems reconfirm you're compliant during a future refresh.

Right around lunchtime today, Google quietly dropped a line onto its search status dashboard: the August 2026 spam update is live. No blog post, no big announcement thread, just a status update and a follow-up LinkedIn post confirming it's "a normal spam update" rolling out globally across all languages. If you weren't watching the volatility trackers, you'd have missed it entirely.

Except a lot of site owners aren't going to miss it, because this is landing in the middle of an already confusing week for Search Console data. More on that in a minute. First, let's talk about what actually changed.

What Actually Happened Today

Google's official line, word for word: "Released the August 2026 spam update, which applies globally and to all languages. The rollout may take a few days to complete." That's it. That's the whole announcement. This is how Google handles most spam updates now — no fanfare, just a dashboard entry, because unlike core updates, spam updates are meant to only affect sites that are actually breaking the rules. In theory, if you're not spamming, you shouldn't feel a thing.

In practice, "collateral damage" is Google's own phrasing, buried in the fine print of basically every spam update announcement going back years. Automated systems misfire. Sites that look spammy but aren't sometimes get caught in the net. If you see a drop this week, it's worth investigating even if you're pretty sure you're clean.

What This Update Targets — and What It Doesn't

This is the part people skip and then get confused about later. Google was explicit that this spam update does not target link spam and does not target the site reputation abuse policy. Those get their own dedicated updates when Google decides to run them. This one is aimed at the broader spam policies — think scaled content abuse, cloaking, doorway pages, expired domain abuse, and similar automated/manipulative tactics that SpamBrain (Google's AI spam detection system) is built to catch.

So if you got hammered by this update and your site doesn't do any of that stuff, but you did just do an aggressive link building push last quarter — this update is probably not why. Look elsewhere. Ranking volatility has other causes, and lumping every traffic dip into "must be the spam update" is a bad habit a lot of site owners have picked up.

SpamBrain keeps getting sharper

Every one of these updates is really just Google tuning SpamBrain to catch new spam patterns it's learned about since the last refresh. That's why spam updates keep happening every few months — it's not that Google suddenly discovered a new category of spam, it's that the detection model got better at recognizing existing categories at scale. Programmatic content mills, AI-generated doorway pages, and expired-domain PBNs are the recurring targets, update after update.

💡 Quick Take

Don't wait for a "did I get hit" panic search on Twitter to check your own data. Pull your Search Console Performance report right now, filter to the last 30 days, and compare week-over-week. If you see a cliff starting around today or tomorrow specifically (not a gradual slide), that's your signal to dig deeper.

Why the Timing Is Extra Confusing Right Now

Here's the wrinkle that's going to cause a lot of unnecessary panic this week: Search Console has had a separate, unrelated bug since around August 12-13. Site owners started reporting a sharp drop in impressions in both the regular Performance report and the Generative AI Features report — in some cases a "cliff-edge" drop within a couple of days, across totally unrelated properties at once.

John Mueller confirmed on Bluesky that this is "just a logging issue and not representative of visibility changes in Search," and Google added it to its official data anomalies page as a logging error affecting the Generative AI performance report starting August 13, plus a related issue hitting Discover impressions and clicks the same day.

So you've now got two separate things happening in the same week: a real spam update that can genuinely tank rankings for sites that violate policy, and a data logging bug that makes your dashboards look worse than reality even if nothing changed. Mixing these up is exactly how people end up chasing ghosts — rewriting content or disavowing links to fix a problem that was never a ranking problem in the first place.

How to Tell If You Actually Got Hit

Before you do anything drastic, run through this checklist:

  • Check actual rankings, not just Search Console impressions. Use a rank tracker or manually check a handful of your target keywords. If positions haven't moved but impressions dropped, that's the logging bug, not a penalty.
  • Look at Google Analytics traffic alongside GSC. Several site owners this week reported GSC impressions cratering while GA sessions from organic search stayed flat. That mismatch is a strong tell it's the reporting bug, not a real hit.
  • Check the timing precisely. The spam update went live around 12:30pm ET today, August 18. If your drop started before that — even a day or two before — it's not this update. The GSC logging bug started around August 12-13, a full week earlier.
  • Run a site: search for spammy-looking pages. If you inherited spam from a hacked plugin, an old subdomain, or a forgotten section of the site, this is a good time to find it before Google's refresh cycle locks in a lower trust score.
  • Cross-reference volatility trackers. Tools like Semrush Sensor, Mozcast, and AccuRanker's Grump index all track industry-wide ranking volatility. If they're spiking today across the board and you're seeing a drop too, that's more consistent with the spam update than a site-specific issue.

The Recovery Timeline Nobody Wants to Hear

If you genuinely got hit because your site was doing something against Google's spam policies, here's the reality check: Google says it can take many months to recover, and recovery only happens when Google's automated systems reconfirm compliance during a future refresh cycle. There's no manual reconsideration request for algorithmic spam updates the way there is for manual actions. You fix the issue, you wait, and eventually — if you're clean — your visibility comes back.

The one caveat that matters most: if this were a link spam update specifically, Google is upfront that fixing your links afterward often doesn't help, because the ranking benefit those links generated is just gone once the system discounts them. That's not the case here since Google explicitly said link spam isn't the target this time, but it's worth remembering for the next update that is.

What to Do This Week

Practically speaking, here's the order of operations I'd follow if I were managing a site through this:

  1. Don't touch anything reactively today. Let the rollout finish — Google said a few days — before drawing conclusions from day-one data.
  2. Separate the GSC logging bug from real ranking changes using the checklist above.
  3. Audit your own site honestly against the spam policies page. Scaled content abuse and doorway pages are the two most common ways sites accidentally cross the line without realizing it, especially if you've been leaning on AI content tools without editorial review.
  4. If you find something, fix it now rather than waiting. The sooner you're clean, the sooner a future refresh can pick that up.
  5. Document what you're seeing. If you're an agency or managing multiple sites, keep a simple log of which properties moved and when — it'll save you a headache when a client asks "did we get hit" three months from now.

Bottom Line

This is a routine spam update, not a core update, so most sites reading this won't feel it at all. But between the update itself and an unrelated GSC data bug hitting at almost the same time, this is a week where jumping to conclusions is easy and expensive. Check your rankings, not just your dashboards. Confirm the timing before you assume causation. And if you do find real spam issues on your site, fix them and settle in — this isn't a fast fix, it's a patience game.
