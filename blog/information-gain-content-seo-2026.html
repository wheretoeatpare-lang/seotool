Information Gain: The Google Signal That's Silently Crushing Copycat Content

RS
James Reyes
RankSorcery Team
· 📅 July 4, 2026 · ⏱ 11 min read · 👁 1,847 views

I had a client earlier this year who was doing everything "right." Great domain authority. Solid backlinks. Content that was technically well-written and covered the topic thoroughly. But their traffic had been trending down since the March 2026 core update, and nobody could figure out why.

When I actually read through their blog, I immediately saw the problem — and I kicked myself for not seeing it sooner. Every article they published was essentially a polished remix of the top three ranking pages. Good structure, clean prose, accurate information. But nothing you couldn't already find on the first page of Google. No original data. No contrarian angle. No stories from working clients. No specific examples that weren't also plastered across competitor content.

That's the Information Gain problem in a nutshell. And in 2026, it's getting expensive.

What Information Gain Actually Is

Google has held a patent on an "Information Gain" scoring model since around 2020. For most of that time, it was treated as an interesting technical footnote — one of those signals that existed in theory but was hard to detect in practice. The March 2026 core update changed that. Multiple credible sources in the SEO community, including data from sites that saw dramatic rank swings, point to Information Gain being significantly re-weighted.

Here's the basic idea: Google scores your content not just on whether it covers a topic, but on how much new, useful information it contributes relative to what already exists in the top results. If the top five pages all say the same five things, and your page also says those same five things (even better), your Information Gain score is low. You're just adding noise to a crowded frequency.

💡
The Core Principle Google isn't just asking "Is this content good?" It's asking "Does this content add something the existing results don't already have?" Those are very different questions — and most content fails the second one completely.

The system works a bit like information theory: a piece of content that confirms what users already know from other sources carries low information value. Content that introduces genuinely new angles, original data, first-hand experience, or perspectives that diverge from the consensus carries high information value. The model rewards the latter even when the former is technically more polished.

Why Most Content Fails the Information Gain Test

This is where I get a little uncomfortable, because I've written content that fails this test too. The incentives in content marketing have historically pushed toward comprehensiveness and keyword density. Cover everything. Include every subtopic. Make it the definitive guide. Hit your word count. The problem is that "comprehensive" and "original" aren't the same thing.

Here's a quick breakdown of the most common failure modes:

The Best-Of Aggregate

You research the top-ranking pages, identify all the subtopics they cover, then write a piece that covers all of them — but doesn't add anything those pages don't already say. This is probably the most common content pattern on the internet right now, and it's the one Information Gain hits hardest. The irony is that this style of content was rewarded for years. Comprehensive beats thin content. Not anymore, at least not on its own.

The Generic How-To

"How to do X in 2026" articles that give the same five steps every other article gives are dead weight to Information Gain systems. If your step-by-step process for setting up a canonical tag is identical to what you'd find in three other guides, you're not adding new information — you're just another voice in the echo chamber. Google increasingly doesn't need more voices saying the same thing.

AI-Generated Paraphrasing

This one stings, because it's where a lot of content teams ended up in 2025. Using AI to produce content that rephrases existing sources at scale creates a massive Information Gain deficit. You're not generating original insights — you're processing and reformatting what's already indexed. Google is quite good at detecting this pattern, and the June 2026 spam update specifically targeted sites doing it at volume.

⚠️
The AI Content Trap AI-generated content that simply rephrases what's already ranking is literally the opposite of what Information Gain rewards. The signal isn't about whether AI wrote the content — it's about whether the content introduces something genuinely new. AI can help you write faster, but it can't replace original thinking, first-hand data, or real experience.

What High Information Gain Actually Looks Like

I want to be specific here, because "be original" is advice so vague it's almost useless. Here's what actually registers as high Information Gain in practice:

1

Original Data and Research

Your own surveys, tests, experiments, or analysis. A case study from a client. A/B test results from your own campaigns. Data you collected that nobody else has. This is the clearest signal of information gain — you're the only source for this information.

2

First-Person Experience Specific Enough to Be Verifiable

Not "I've found that internal linking helps rankings" — that's generic. But "after adding 3–5 contextual internal links to every new post on a SaaS client's blog, their crawl depth dropped from 4.2 to 2.1 pages over 6 weeks" — that's a specific, experience-backed claim nobody else made.

3

Contrarian or Nuanced Takes Backed by Evidence

If everyone says "publish long-form content," and you have data showing that in your niche 800-word posts actually outperform 3,000-word ones, that's high Information Gain territory. Disagreeing with consensus is valuable — as long as you show your work.

4

Novel Frameworks or Structures

A new way of categorizing or explaining something. A framework you built from your own experience that doesn't exist anywhere else. These don't require data — they require genuine thinking about a problem.

5

Gaps in the Existing Coverage

Look at the top results and find what nobody's saying. Not because it's wrong, but because everyone assumes the reader knows it, or because nobody's bothered. Filling that gap is legitimate Information Gain.

"The question isn't 'Is this a good article?' — it's 'Does this article say anything the existing top results don't already say?' Most content can't answer yes."

How to Audit Your Existing Content for Information Gain

The practical audit isn't complicated, but it is tedious — and most teams skip it. Here's how I approach it:

  • Pull your top 20 pages by organic impressions from Search Console
  • For each page, search Google for your target keyword and open the top 5 results
  • Read each competing page and list every distinct claim, example, or data point they make
  • Compare your page against that list — mark every point your page makes that also appears in 3+ competitors
  • Anything that overlaps with 3+ competitors is low Information Gain territory
  • Flag pages where more than 60% of your content points are duplicated across competitors — these need revision
  • Identify what unique claims, data, or perspectives your page makes that competitors don't
  • Check whether your unique elements are buried (low on the page) or front-loaded — they should be early

The most common finding when I do this audit: the unique stuff exists, but it's in the last 30% of the article. Google's systems weight early signals more heavily. If your most original content is in paragraph 14, you're effectively hiding it.

🔍
Quick Audit Trick Copy the first 200 words of your article. Paste them into a search. If you see near-identical phrasing across competitor content, your opening — the highest-weighted section for Information Gain — is failing. Rewrite the intro to lead with your most original observation or data point.

Rewriting Existing Content for Information Gain

Not every low-performing article needs to be scrapped. A lot of it just needs to be restructured and enriched. The goal is to shift the ratio of unique content versus recycled content in your favor. Here's the approach I use:

Lead with your angle, not the definition. Almost every SEO article still opens by defining the keyword — "keyword cannibalization is when multiple pages on your site target the same keyword." Everyone knows. Nobody needs that in 2026. Open with your specific observation, a surprising data point, or a problem most people are getting wrong.

Add a section that doesn't exist anywhere else. Look at your competitor pages and find the thing everyone mentions but nobody explains. If everyone says "fix your internal links" but nobody explains what a bad anchor text distribution actually looks like in real data, write that section. That's your Information Gain.

Share the uncomfortable part. What didn't work? What surprised you? What would you do differently? The messy, honest stuff is almost always unique because most content is written to sound authoritative, not honest.

Information Gain in the Context of AI Search

Here's where things get particularly interesting for 2026: Information Gain matters twice as much if you want to be cited in AI Overviews or Perplexity-style answers.

AI systems synthesizing answers from multiple sources explicitly favor content that says something different from the crowd. If all sources say the same thing, the AI doesn't need to cite all of them — it picks one or two. But if your content introduces a specific data point, a unique framing, or a perspective that adds to the answer rather than repeating it, you become more citable. You're serving a function in the synthesis that nobody else is.

Research from early 2026 shows that pages with high semantic uniqueness scores — meaning their content diverges meaningfully from competing pages — get cited in AI Overviews at roughly 7× the rate of pages with low uniqueness scores, even when the lower-uniqueness pages rank higher in traditional results.

🤖
AI Citation Signal Traditional rankings and AI citations are starting to decouple. A page can rank #6 in traditional results but get cited consistently in AI Overviews because it contributes something no higher-ranking page does. Information Gain is the bridge between those two systems.

What You Should Actually Do About This

I'm not going to tell you to "audit your entire content library" because you're not going to do that, and it's not the right starting point anyway. Here's what's actually worth doing in priority order:

  • Pick your top 5 traffic pages and read them against competitors right now — these have the most to lose and the most to gain
  • Add one unique section to each — your real experience, a specific example, or data only you have
  • Rewrite the first 200 words to lead with your most original claim or observation
  • Stop publishing content that's just better-written versions of what already ranks — quality alone isn't enough anymore
  • Build a "unique angle" step into your content brief — before writing, ask: what will this say that the top 5 results don't?
  • Use original data where possible — even small surveys, your own test results, or client examples qualify
  • Track AI Overview citations separately from rankings — they're increasingly different signals

A Note on Scale

If you're running a content-heavy site and worried about the sheer volume of existing articles that might fail an Information Gain audit — that's a real concern. The honest answer is that you can't fix everything at once. Prioritize by traffic potential, not by publish date. A two-year-old article that still drives 3,000 clicks a month is worth more attention than five newer articles combined.

And for new content going forward: just make "what's the unique angle here?" a mandatory question before you greenlight any piece. That single question, asked consistently, will produce better results than almost any other process change you can make.

🎯
The Bottom Line Google's Information Gain signal rewards content that adds something genuinely new to what exists online. Polished, comprehensive, keyword-optimized content that covers the same ground as your competitors is increasingly a losing bet. The sites winning in 2026 are the ones with something specific and real to say — not just something well-written.

One final thing I'll say: this is actually good news if you're willing to do the work. It means the playing field is tilting back toward people with real expertise and real experience, and away from whoever can spin up the most AI-generated comprehensive guides. That's a better internet, and it rewards the SEOs and content teams willing to think, not just produce.
