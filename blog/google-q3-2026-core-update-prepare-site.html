This article is about what to do in that window. Not theory, not generic "create good content" advice. Specific things you can check, fix, and improve before the next wave hits — so you're the one who comes out of Q3 2026 in better shape than when you went in.

What We Know About the Q3 2026 Core Update

Let me be straight with you: nobody knows exactly when the next core update drops. Google doesn't announce these things in advance, and anyone telling you they have inside information is making things up. What we do know is the pattern.

Google ran confirmed broad core updates in 2026 in January and May. The May update finished rolling out by early June, and Google's guidance says to wait for a "clean comparison window" of several weeks before drawing conclusions from Search Console data. That window is now open, and the next update — based on every pattern we've seen since 2019 — is most likely August or September.

The July 2026 SEO news has been relatively quiet on the core update front, but that doesn't mean Google has been idle. There's been significant activity on spam enforcement — particularly around what Google publicly called "Parasite SEO" in an official blog post — and SERP features have continued evolving fast. The AI Overview footprint alone has grown from roughly 30% to 48% of tracked queries over the past year. That's not a minor tweak; that's a restructuring of how search results work.

Here's what that means practically: even if you survived the May 2026 core update without losing rankings, you might still be in a weaker position than six months ago — because the value of those rankings has changed. Being position one below an AI Overview is not the same as being position one on a traditional SERP. You could be holding the same rank while watching your actual traffic decline. That's the part most dashboards don't show you cleanly.

💡
Pro Tip Don't just check your rankings before the next update — check your actual clicks and impressions in Google Search Console. If impressions are up but clicks are down on the same keywords, AI Overviews are probably eating your traffic. That's a separate problem from ranking, and fixing it requires a different approach than traditional SEO.

What Google Has Been Targeting in 2026 (So Far)

Understanding what previous updates targeted is genuinely useful preparation, because Google is not random. There are recurring themes, and they've been getting more aggressive about enforcing them. The 2026 updates have been consistent about a few specific things.

First: content that exists primarily for search engines, not people. This sounds obvious — Google has been saying this for years — but the enforcement has gotten much sharper. Sites that built large libraries of thin, keyword-optimized content and saw early gains from it have been getting pruned. Not all of them, and not instantly, but steadily. If you have pages that rank but don't actually answer the question a searcher is asking, those are your highest-risk pages going into Q3.

Second: Parasite SEO. Google officially went public about cracking down on what they called "Parasite SEO spam" — where content gets published on high-authority domains to borrow their ranking power for entirely unrelated topics. Think sponsored posts on news sites ranking for gambling or finance terms. Google named this pattern directly in an official blog post, which is unusual and signals they're putting real engineering resources behind the enforcement. If you've built any backlink strategy around this type of placement, now is the time to reassess it.

Third: E-E-A-T signals that are easy to fake versus signals that aren't. Experience and authoritativeness signals that can be verified externally — author bylines with real history, citations from other respected sites, brand mentions on third-party platforms — are weighted more heavily than on-site claims. Anyone can write "Our team of experts" in a paragraph. Fewer sites have actual traceable expertise signals that Google can verify independently.

The AI Citation Factor Nobody Is Talking About

There's a fourth pattern worth calling out separately, because it's newer and most guides aren't covering it yet. Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode are pulling from a different source pool than traditional rankings. Semrush data from 2025 showed that pages not updated at least quarterly are three times more likely to lose AI citations compared to freshly updated content. This is separate from whether you rank — it's about whether you get cited in the AI summary at the top of the page.

Why does this matter for core update prep? Because Google's broad core updates increasingly evaluate not just whether a page ranks, but whether it's a source that the system trusts enough to surface in AI-generated answers. The two systems — traditional ranking and AI citation — are being evaluated with more overlap than most people realize. Stale content hurts you on both fronts.

I've seen sites with pages that haven't been touched since 2024 and are still ranking on page one. That's fine — for now. But they're invisible in AI Overviews, and when the Q3 update hits, those pages are running without a safety net. The fact that they've survived this long is more about Google's update cadence than about those pages being in good shape.

⚠️
Warning Sign If you're seeing steady or rising impressions in Search Console but declining clicks, and you haven't updated those pages in 6+ months, you are almost certainly losing AI Overview citations. This will get worse, not better, after the next core update.

The Pre-Update Site Audit: What to Check Right Now

The single most useful thing you can do in the next two weeks is a thorough technical and content audit. Not a surface-level look — a real one, where you go through your Search Console data, crawl your site, and identify the pages that are dragging down your site's overall quality signal.

Google evaluates sites holistically during core updates. That's not marketing language — it's been confirmed repeatedly in their guidance. A cluster of thin, outdated, or low-quality pages on your domain affects how the algorithm evaluates your other pages, even the good ones. I've personally seen sites where removing 15–20% of their content — pages that were getting zero traffic and had little substance — produced ranking improvements for the pages that remained. The math is counterintuitive but real.

Here's what to specifically look for:

  • Pages with impressions but near-zero clicks — Check Search Console for pages getting impressions but a CTR below 0.5%. These might be ranking for irrelevant queries or have titles that don't match searcher intent.
  • Content older than 12 months with no updates — Especially for topics where information changes (algorithm guides, pricing pages, statistics-heavy posts). Stale content is triple the risk for AI Overview citations.
  • Pages with thin content — Under 400 words with no real depth, no examples, no original insight. These are the pages most likely to be filtered out in a quality recalibration.
  • Broken internal links and 404 errors — A crawl will surface these. Every broken link is a signal of site hygiene issues that Google does factor into trust scoring.
  • Core Web Vitals failures — LCP (Largest Contentful Paint) above 2.5 seconds, CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift) above 0.1, INP above 200ms. Check your Search Console Core Web Vitals report and fix any pages marked "Poor."
  • Duplicate or near-duplicate content — Including pages that cover the same topic with only slight variation. These dilute your topical authority instead of building it.
  • Missing or weak author information — For any content where expertise matters (health, finance, legal, technical), Google looks for verifiable author credentials. An anonymous byline is a trust gap.

What to Do With What You Find

Once you have your audit results, you're going to face a decision on every underperforming page: improve it, merge it with a better page, or remove it. There's no fourth option that works. Leaving problem pages as-is and hoping they'll be fine is how sites get surprised by core updates.

The decision framework I use: if a page gets zero organic traffic and has less than 300 words with no unique insight, remove it or redirect it to a more comprehensive page. If it gets some traffic but the content is outdated, update it — actually update it, not just change the date at the top. If it covers a topic that overlaps heavily with another page, pick the stronger one and merge them.

This is not a fun process. It takes time. But it is far better to do this work now, on your terms, than to do it as an emergency response after rankings drop and revenue follows.

Content and E-E-A-T: Where Most Sites Will Win or Lose

Let me be honest with you about something: E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness) has become one of those terms that everyone uses and almost nobody implements correctly. It's not a checklist you complete once. It's a set of signals that Google triangulates from multiple sources — your site, third-party mentions of your brand and authors, and how your content compares to what else exists on the topic.

The Experience component — the first E, added in late 2022 — is the one most sites are still underweighting. Google wants to see evidence that the person writing about a topic has actually dealt with it in the real world. Not just researched it. Lived it, tried it, failed at it, figured it out. First-person examples, specific scenarios, case studies from real situations — these are the signals that differentiate original experience from research-based summaries.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: a lot of SEO content is research-based summaries. Even good SEO content. It synthesizes information from other sources, organizes it well, and is technically accurate. But it's not experiential. And in 2026, as AI gets better at producing exactly that type of synthesis-based content, the thing that keeps human-written content valuable is the specific, lived experience that AI can't replicate. That's your competitive edge, and it needs to show up explicitly in your writing.

"The sites that will survive Q3 2026 aren't the ones with the most content — they're the ones with content that could only have come from someone who actually knows the subject."

On the Authoritativeness front: who is linking to you, who mentions your brand, what forums and communities reference your site — these external signals carry more weight than what you say about yourself on your own site. If your About page says you're an authority but you have zero coverage in third-party editorial sources and no brand mentions anywhere outside your own domain, that's a credibility gap that on-page optimization cannot fix.

One often-overlooked way to build these external signals is through competitor research. Understanding which sites are linking to your competitors but not to you shows you exactly where to focus your outreach. You don't need to build links to every page — you need the right links to your highest-value pages. A good competitor analysis before the core update can give you a prioritized list of where to spend your effort in the next four to six weeks.

The Freshness Signal Is Bigger Than You Think

Content freshness has always mattered for time-sensitive topics. What's changed in 2026 is that it now matters for almost everything, because of AI Overviews. When Google's AI generates a summary, it draws from sources that are both authoritative and recent. Semrush data showed that pages with no updates in over three months are three times more likely to be dropped from AI citations compared to regularly updated pages. That's a significant multiplier on the risk of inaction.

What counts as a meaningful update? Not changing the date. Not tweaking a word or two. A real update means: adding new data, expanding a section that's become outdated, incorporating new examples that have emerged since the original publication, or addressing questions that commenters or search queries have raised since the page went live. Google's systems are sophisticated enough to differentiate between a cosmetic refresh and actual content improvement.

✅
What Makes an Update Count Adding new statistics or research, expanding thin sections with specific examples, addressing new questions that have emerged in your niche, updating outdated recommendations, or adding a new perspective based on developments since original publication. Any one of these signals genuine content improvement to Google's systems.

Your 4-Week Action Plan Before the Update Hits

If the next core update lands in August or September — which is the most probable window based on every pattern we have — you have somewhere between four and ten weeks right now. That's not a lot of time if you're trying to do everything. It's enough time to do the most important things.

Here's how I'd structure it if I were preparing a site right now. Week one is entirely about assessment — you can't prioritize without knowing what you're working with. Weeks two and three are execution on the highest-impact items. Week four is monitoring and adjusting based on what's moved.

1

Run a Full Technical + Content Audit

Use RankSorcery's SEO Auditor to crawl your site and flag technical issues, then pull your Search Console data to identify pages with declining clicks, low CTR, or zero traffic. Build a prioritized list of pages that need action before week two starts.

2

Refresh or Remove Your 10 Weakest Pages

Don't try to fix everything at once. Pick the ten pages that are either most at risk (thin, stale, duplicate) or getting the most impressions with poor engagement, and either meaningfully update them or redirect them to stronger pages. Ten pages done properly beats fifty pages touched superficially.

3

Audit Your Competitor Backlink Profile

Use RankSorcery's Competitor Analyzer to identify which domains are linking to your top competitors but not to you. These are qualified link opportunities — sites that have already shown they'll cover your topic — and reaching out now gives you a few weeks for those links to be indexed before the update.

4

Check Your AI Search Visibility

Traditional rankings tell you where you appear in blue-link results, but that's only half the picture now. Use RankSorcery's AI Search Ranking tool to see how often your site is being cited or mentioned in AI-generated search results across major platforms. If you're invisible in AI results for your core topics, that's a content and authority gap that needs to be addressed before it becomes a traffic problem.

One thing I want to say clearly: don't try to optimize for the core update in the sense of guessing what signals will be rewarded. Google has been saying for years that there's no "fix" for a core update — the fix is having a genuinely high-quality site. What this preparation is really doing is surfacing the places where your site has quality gaps and giving you the time to address them. If you do that, you're not trying to game anything. You're just improving.

The sites that panic after updates and start making rapid, reactive changes often end up worse. The sites that come out ahead are usually the ones that were doing steady maintenance anyway — updating content regularly, fixing technical issues as they appeared, building real authority through genuine content. A core update is Google recalibrating toward that kind of site. Be that kind of site going into August, and you'll be fine.

If you're not sure where to start, start with the audit. Everything else flows from knowing exactly what you're dealing with. A five-minute audit now is worth twenty hours of reactive scrambling after a ranking drop.
