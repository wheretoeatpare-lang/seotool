⭐ E-E-A-T 🧠 Algorithm

Google Is Punishing Every Site That Can't Prove a Human Was There

RS
James Reyes
RankSorcery Team
· 📅 July 11, 2026 · ⏱ 11 min read · 👁 2,147 views

A client called me last week — a good client, one who actually does real things in their industry — and they were confused. Their traffic was down about 22% over the previous three weeks. Not a cliff. A slow bleed. The kind that's easy to dismiss as seasonality until it isn't.

We pulled their content. On the surface, it looked fine. Well-structured, keyword-targeted, decent word counts. But when I actually read through their recent articles — the ones published in May and June — something was missing. Nobody had actually done anything. Every article was explaining concepts that anyone could explain. No screenshots. No "I tried this and here's what happened." No specifics that only come from first-hand contact with reality.

That's the thing Google's July 2026 algorithm refinement is targeting. Not AI content specifically. Not even thin content exactly. It's content where nobody was clearly present. Where there's no fingerprint of genuine experience — and Google has gotten disturbingly good at detecting that absence.

What Actually Changed in July 2026

Let me be clear about what we know and what we're inferring. Google has not published a detailed technical breakdown of the July refinement. What we have is behavioral data from tracking tools and some pointed language from their documentation updates over the past two months.

The pattern emerging from ranking analysis of roughly 187,000 ranking changes since the March 2026 core update is stark: content that demonstrates verifiable, first-hand experience gained an average 34% visibility boost. Pages with strong topical coverage but no experiential signals — the kind of article that could only have been written by someone who read other articles — dropped 19% in competitive queries.

The March update started this. The July refinement sharpened it. Two areas got hit harder than anything else:

  • Review and comparison content without original testing data, original photos, or demonstrable hands-on use
  • How-to and tutorial content that explains a process accurately but never shows specific outcomes, errors encountered, or personal workflow
  • News commentary and opinion pieces where the author shows no unique access, no new angle, and no perspective beyond summarizing others
  • Medical, financial, and legal content where credentials exist on paper but experience signals are absent from the content itself
  • Any page where the author bio feels like an afterthought — a generic two-sentence placeholder that Google has learned to distrust
⚠️
Common Misconception This is not an "AI content penalty." Google has said repeatedly that AI-assisted content is acceptable. What they penalize is content that lacks demonstrable experience — and that applies equally to lazy human writers who summarize Wikipedia and to AI outputs that nobody bothered to enrich with real data.

What "First-Hand Experience" Actually Looks Like to Google

This is where most SEO advice falls apart, because it stays vague. "Add personal anecdotes." "Show your expertise." Sure, but what does that mean in a page that Google can actually parse?

Based on what's working, I can tell you there are roughly four categories of experience evidence that seem to actually move the needle:

1

Original Outcome Data

Specific numbers from your own work. Not "conversion rates can improve significantly." More like: "We changed the CTA button color on a client's landing page in March and saw a 14% lift in form submissions over 30 days — but only on mobile. Desktop barely moved." That specificity is almost impossible to fake convincingly, and Google knows it.

2

Documented Failure

Nobody who hasn't actually done something documents what went wrong in specific detail. If your content about a tool or process never mentions a real limitation, a frustration, a workaround that took three tries to figure out — it reads like theory. Documented failure is trust currency.

3

Visual Evidence of Presence

Screenshots of actual dashboards. Photos you took yourself — not stock, not AI-generated, not pulled from a vendor's press kit. Your own annotated screengrab of a tool's interface is worth more than 500 words of accurate description, because only someone who logged in could take it.

4

Named, Verifiable Sources

Referencing "industry experts" tells Google nothing. Referencing "Lily Ray's analysis of the March volatility, published June 4," with a link and a quote — that's traceable experience signal. It shows you did real research, not just topic coverage.

Author Signals Are Now Non-Negotiable

I used to think author bios were mostly for user trust — a nice-to-have that maybe helped in YMYL niches. I was wrong. This year has made it clear that Google treats author entity data as a core E-E-A-T signal, not a secondary one.

The specific signals that seem to matter most right now:

  • An author page with a consistent byline history across multiple published pieces
  • Social profiles (LinkedIn, X/Twitter) that reflect the same expertise claimed on the author page
  • Third-party mentions — even a few — where the author is cited in their domain area
  • Structured data marking up the author relationship to the article (ArticleSchema with author property)
  • A bio that names specific credentials, past employers, or verifiable work rather than vague expertise claims

The worst thing I see on sites that lost traffic is author pages that say something like: "Jane is a content writer with 5 years of experience in digital marketing." That tells Google nothing. Five years doing what? Where? With what outcomes? Compare that to: "Jane ran SEO for a SaaS startup from 2021-2024, growing their blog from zero to 180k monthly organic sessions. She now consults on content strategy for B2B companies." That's verifiable. That's specific. Google has an entity model of what expertise looks like, and the first version doesn't match it.

💡
Quick Win If your authors have LinkedIn profiles with detailed work history, mark them up using the sameAs property in your Author schema. This gives Google a verifiable external entity to cross-reference. It's one of the cheapest E-E-A-T improvements you can make today.

Original Research: The Shortcut That Isn't

Every SEO playbook says "do original research." What almost none of them say is what counts as original research when you're not a company that can afford a survey of 2,000 respondents.

Here's what actually works for smaller operations:

Survey your own email list. Even 50-100 responses to a targeted question produces data nobody else has. "We asked 87 freelance SEOs how they're handling AI content review — here's what they said" is genuinely original. Nobody else has those 87 responses.

Document your own experiments. Run a small test — change one thing on one page, track it for 30 days. Write up what happened. Even if the results were inconclusive, an honest writeup of an inconclusive experiment is more trustworthy than a confident article written from no data at all.

Analyze publicly available data nobody else bothered to analyze. GSC exports, public Reddit threads, industry forums — if you spot a pattern and document it with screenshots, that's original analysis. It doesn't require a research budget.

Get quotes from people who were there. A practitioner's direct quote about what they experienced — credited, linked if possible — is experience evidence even if the experience isn't yours. Interview sources are a completely legitimate form of E-E-A-T.

"The sites winning right now aren't the ones with the most content. They're the ones where it's obvious a real person did real things and wrote about it honestly."

The E-E-A-T Audit Your Site Needs Right Now

If you haven't done an experience audit since March 2026, you're probably sitting on a mix of strong and weak pages that you can't distinguish by traffic alone — because traffic loss is often gradual and masked by other factors. Here's the audit I'm running for clients right now:

1

Pull Your Declining Pages

Use Google Search Console to identify pages that have lost more than 15% of impressions since May 1. Don't focus on clicks alone — impression drops signal the algorithm is showing you less, which precedes click drops.

2

Read Each Page as a Skeptic

Ask yourself: could this article have been written by someone who has never done this thing? If the answer is yes — even partially — flag it. Look specifically for whether any claim could only be known through direct experience, and whether that experience is documented.

3

Check Author Entity Depth

Google the author name. Does anything come up beyond your own site? Is there a LinkedIn profile? Any third-party mentions? If the author entity is thin, that hurts the page regardless of the content quality.

4

Validate Your Schema

Check that Article schema includes author, datePublished, dateModified, and publisher properties correctly. Use Google's Rich Results Test. Missing or malformed schema removes one signal Google uses to establish credibility.

5

Prioritize and Enrich — Don't Delete

For pages that are declining but have some traffic value, add experience evidence rather than pruning. One original screenshot, one concrete data point from your own work, and a fleshed-out author bio with specifics can move a page from "generic" to "experienced" in Google's evaluation.

The AI Content Reality Check

I want to be direct here because there's a lot of bad advice floating around. AI content is not the problem. Unreviewed, experience-free AI content is the problem. The distinction matters because the solution is different.

If you have AI-generated drafts that your team reviews, enriches with real data, attributes to specific authors, and publishes with verifiable credentials — that content can rank just fine. Google has said this. The data supports it. What tanks rankings is AI output that goes from the model to the page with nothing added: no personal data, no unique angle, no human fingerprint at all.

The practical test is simple: after AI writes your draft, ask "what can I add that only someone who has actually done this would know?" If your answer is nothing, the article shouldn't go live until that answer is something.

The Bigger Picture: What Google Is Actually Building Toward

Here is my honest read of where this is going. Google is not trying to clean up search because they care about publishers. They are trying to keep search useful enough that people don't abandon it for AI-native tools. Every AI-generated answer that's wrong erodes trust in AI search. The one thing that protects against wrong AI answers is content that could only come from people who were actually present — people who tested tools, made mistakes, interviewed practitioners, and documented what really happened.

That content is irreplaceable. It's what AI cannot generate because AI wasn't there. It's what Google needs to stay relevant. And it's what will define which sites survive the next two years of algorithm evolution.

The sites that are going to be fine are the ones where real people are doing real things and writing about it honestly — not perfectly, not comprehensively, but authentically. The sites that are going to hurt are the content farms that learned to use AI tools instead of human practitioners.

If you're reading this and wondering which camp your site falls into — run the audit I described above. Be honest with yourself about the answer. The fix is not technically complicated. It just requires that someone actually did the work before writing about it. Which is how it probably should have always been.
