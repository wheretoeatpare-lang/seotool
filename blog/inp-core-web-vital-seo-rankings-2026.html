What INP Actually Is (And Why It's Not Just FID with a New Name)

The old metric, First Input Delay (FID), measured how long it took the browser to start processing the very first interaction a user made on your page. Sounds useful, right? Here's the problem: it only measured the first interaction, and it only measured the delay to start processing — not how long the actual processing took. So you could have a page where the first click responded instantly but every subsequent click was sluggish and laggy. FID would report a perfect score. Your users would hate the experience. Google was essentially measuring the wrong thing.

INP fixes this. Instead of looking at just the first interaction, INP measures all interactions on the page — every click, tap, and keyboard input — throughout the entire page lifecycle. It finds the worst-performing interaction (or close to it — there's a statistical adjustment for outliers) and reports that as your INP score. So if your page loads fast and feels snappy until the user scrolls down and hits a filter button in a product listing, and that filter takes 600ms to respond, your INP score reflects that real-world lag.

The thresholds are strict. To pass, your INP needs to be under 200 milliseconds. Between 200ms and 500ms is "Needs Improvement." Over 500ms is flat-out "Poor." And these aren't measured in lab conditions — Google uses real Chrome user data (the Chrome User Experience Report, or CrUX) to calculate your score. That means it's based on how actual visitors are experiencing your site right now, on real devices, on real network connections. There's no gaming it with a fast test machine in ideal conditions.

💡
Quick Check Open Google Search Console and go to Core Web Vitals. If you see a red or orange status for "Needs Improvement" or "Poor" pages, click through to see which metric is causing the issue. A lot of sites currently failing are failing specifically on INP, especially on mobile. This is your starting point before you do anything else.

One thing worth understanding: INP uses a 75th percentile measurement. That means 75% of your real users need to experience an INP under 200ms for you to have a "Good" score. If you have a fast site for most users but terrible performance for 30% of visitors (maybe those on slower phones or connections), you can still fail. This is actually more representative of reality than most performance testing tools show you.

Why Google Made INP a Star Ranking Signal in 2026

Google has been very clear for years that user experience signals matter for rankings. But for a long time, "user experience" in the algorithmic sense was pretty loosely defined. Core Web Vitals changed that by giving Google concrete, measurable signals tied directly to how users perceive page quality. INP is the signal most directly tied to how interactive and responsive a page feels — which is increasingly important as websites have gotten more complex and JavaScript-heavy.

Here's where 2026 gets interesting. The March 2026 core update — which was confirmed by multiple SEO tracking tools to have caused significant ranking volatility — specifically increased the weighting of Core Web Vitals signals. Research published after the update showed that 18% of observed ranking changes correlated with CWV score changes. That's a substantial correlation for a technical signal. And among the three CWV metrics (LCP, INP, CLS), INP is the one most sites were already struggling with going into the update.

What's driving this? A few things. First, the web has gotten heavier. The median page weight across all websites is now around 2.3MB, with JavaScript payloads averaging 468KB — a number that has ballooned over the past few years as frameworks and third-party tools have multiplied. More JavaScript means more processing, more processing means slower interaction responses, slower interaction responses means poor INP. Second, mobile traffic now accounts for 62% of all web visits, and mobile devices are significantly more resource-constrained than desktops. A page that responds in 80ms on a MacBook might respond in 400ms on a budget Android phone. Google's field data captures both.

There's also a competitive angle that a lot of people are sleeping on. If 58% of sites are failing Core Web Vitals on mobile, that means there's a real opportunity for sites that pass to pull ahead. Google doesn't rank you against an absolute standard — it ranks you relative to your competitors. If your competitors' sites are sluggish and yours is snappy, that difference is now baked into the algorithm in a way it wasn't as heavily weighted two years ago.

If you can get your INP into the green while they're still sitting at 400ms, you're picking up a ranking signal advantage they're not even aware of yet.

The Real Culprits Destroying Your INP Score

I've seen a lot of sites go through INP audits and there are some patterns that come up almost every single time. The causes aren't mysterious — they're almost always the same handful of things. The frustrating part is that many of them were put there intentionally by marketing or development teams who didn't realize the performance cost they were creating.

JavaScript: The Main Offender

Heavy JavaScript is the number one INP killer, and it's specifically about long tasks. When JavaScript executes a long task on the browser's main thread, it blocks the browser from responding to user input during that time. Click something during a long task? The browser queues that interaction and processes it once the JavaScript finishes. That delay — between when you clicked and when the browser could even start responding — is what INP measures.

The most common culprits are: bloated JavaScript bundles that get downloaded and parsed all at once, third-party scripts (analytics, chat widgets, ad networks, cookie consent tools) that execute on the main thread, and heavy client-side rendering frameworks that do a lot of work on the user's device. If you're running a React or Next.js app with a lot of client-side hydration, you're particularly at risk — that hydration process can block the main thread for 300-800ms on mobile devices.

What makes this especially tricky is that the JavaScript that kills your INP often isn't your own code. It's that marketing pixel someone added six months ago, or the A/B testing script your CRO team installed, or the embedded social media widget in the footer. Each of these adds to main thread contention, and they compound on each other. A site with 8 third-party scripts is not twice as bad as a site with 4 — it can be four or five times worse because the scripts interfere with each other's execution.

Images and Large Media That Block Rendering

This one surprises people. Images don't run JavaScript, so how do they affect INP? The answer is that large, unoptimized images — especially ones loaded without proper sizing attributes — can cause layout shifts and reflows that trigger main thread work. When a browser has to recalculate the layout of a page because an image that was supposed to be 400px wide suddenly loads at a different size, that layout recalculation happens on the main thread and can create exactly the kind of blocking behavior that tanks INP.

Beyond layout issues, large image files can also eat up bandwidth and CPU cycles during decode, which competes with JavaScript execution. On a mobile device where CPU resources are shared, a browser decoding a 4MB uncompressed PNG while also trying to process a click event will always deprioritize the interaction. The image wins, your user waits, your INP score suffers.

The fix here is straightforward but often neglected: compress your images properly, set explicit width and height attributes on all images, and use modern formats like WebP or AVIF that decode faster. If you're running a content-heavy site and you haven't systematically compressed your images, you're almost certainly leaving INP points (and ranking potential) on the table. How to Diagnose Your INP Problem

You can't fix what you can't measure, and the good news is that Google has made INP measurement reasonably accessible without needing to be a performance engineer. Here's where to start.

Google Search Console (field data): This is your ground truth. Go to Core Web Vitals in Search Console and look at both desktop and mobile reports. It shows you which URLs are failing and which metric is the problem. This is based on real user data from Chrome, so it's what Google actually uses for ranking. The downside is that it can take 28 days to reflect changes you make, so it's not great for quick feedback loops.

PageSpeed Insights (lab + field data): Enter any URL and you'll get both lab-measured performance and real-user field data for that specific page. The INP score is shown prominently. More importantly, PageSpeed Insights now shows you the specific interactions that caused the worst INP measurements — so you can see "this was your dropdown menu" instead of just "your INP is 380ms." That specificity is extremely useful for debugging.

Chrome DevTools Performance Panel: If you want to go deep, this is where you do it. Record a session, interact with the page, and you can see exactly which JavaScript tasks are running, how long they take, and whether they're blocking input. Look for "Long Tasks" (anything over 50ms) highlighted in red on the timeline. These are your INP suspects.

Web Vitals Chrome Extension: Install this for quick visual feedback while browsing. It shows INP in real time as you interact with a page, color-coded green/yellow/red. Useful for quickly identifying which interactions on a page are problematic without digging into DevTools every time.

One thing I want to emphasize: look at mobile data specifically. Desktop INP scores are almost always better because desktop CPUs are faster and desktop browsers have more resources. The INP Fix Playbook: Step by Step

Here's the honest reality: fixing INP is not always a quick afternoon project. Some improvements are fast and impactful; others require engineering time and architectural changes. I'll lay out both the quick wins you can get this week and the deeper fixes for sustained improvement.

"The sites that are winning on INP in 2026 aren't the ones with the fanciest frameworks — they're the ones that ruthlessly cut everything the user doesn't actually need to interact with."
1

Audit and Defer Your Third-Party Scripts

Use a tool like Request Map or DebugBear to see every third-party script loading on your page. For each one, ask: does this need to run before the user can interact? Most analytics, social embeds, and marketing pixels don't. Load them with defer or async, or better yet, trigger them after the first user interaction (a technique called "lazy loading on interaction"). Removing or deferring just one heavy third-party script can often improve INP by 100-200ms on mobile.

2

Break Up Long JavaScript Tasks

Any JavaScript task running for more than 50ms on the main thread is a long task. Break these up using setTimeout with zero delay, requestIdleCallback, or the Scheduler API (now well-supported in 2026). Splitting a 300ms task into six 50ms chunks with brief pauses between them allows the browser to process user interactions in those gaps — eliminating the input delay that INP measures.

3

Compress and Properly Size All Your Images

Go through your pages and identify every image that's being loaded at a larger size than it's displayed, and every image without explicit width and height attributes. Set dimensions on every image to prevent layout shifts. Convert large PNGs and JPEGs to WebP — you'll typically cut file sizes by 25-35% with no visible quality loss. Smaller images decode faster, consume less CPU, and compete less with JavaScript for resources. Use RankSorcery's Image Compressor to do this in bulk, and Image Resizer to ensure you're not serving oversized images.

4

Optimize Your Event Handlers

Check the JavaScript that runs when users click or tap things on your page. Event handlers that do heavy synchronous work (DOM manipulation, complex calculations, synchronous API calls) add directly to INP. Move expensive work off the main thread using Web Workers where possible, and avoid triggering synchronous layout thrashing (reading then immediately writing DOM properties in a loop).

5

Implement Code Splitting and Lazy Loading

If you're running a JavaScript framework, ensure you're not loading the entire application bundle on every page. Code splitting means only loading the JavaScript actually needed for the current page, and lazy loading means deferring components and features until the user scrolls to them or triggers them. Reducing the amount of JS that runs on load directly reduces the main thread work that creates long tasks and poor INP scores.

For sites running WordPress, the quick wins are often: deactivating plugins you don't actively need, enabling lazy loading for images (built into WordPress core since 5.5), switching to a faster hosting environment with better server response times, and using a CDN to reduce network latency for visitors who are geographically far from your server.

For ecommerce sites specifically, pay extra attention to product pages with filter and sort interactions, cart and checkout flows, and any page with real-time inventory or pricing updates. These are the interactions that are often the most JavaScript-heavy and the most likely to produce poor INP scores in real-user field data. They're also the pages that matter most for conversions, so fixing them is doubly valuable — better rankings and better user experience in the moments that matter for revenue.

What to Do Right Now

Let me be direct about where to start. Open Google Search Console today. Click on Core Web Vitals. If you have any red or orange pages on mobile, that's your priority list. Don't get distracted by content strategy or backlinks until you know whether a technical problem is actively working against you in rankings.

Next, run your most important pages — homepage, top landing pages, category pages — through PageSpeed Insights on mobile. Look at the INP score. Anything over 200ms deserves attention. Anything over 500ms is actively costing you ranking. Make a list of your worst offenders and use the diagnostics — PageSpeed Insights will tell you which interactions caused the worst INP measurements and give you specific recommendations for what to fix.

Then look at your images. This is often the fastest, easiest win available. Unoptimized images are everywhere, they're fixable with the right tools, and the performance gains compound across your entire site. A few hours of image optimization work can meaningfully move both your INP scores (through reduced decode overhead and layout recalculation) and your Largest Contentful Paint scores at the same time. Use RankSorcery's Image Compressor to compress your images efficiently, and Image Resizer to make sure you're not serving a 2000px wide image in a 400px slot.

Finally — don't expect overnight results in Search Console. Field data takes time to accumulate and Google's ranking systems incorporate it over weeks, not days. The 34% of sites that saw ranking gains after CWV improvements in 2026 saw those gains within 28 days. That's a realistic timeline. Do the work now, track your changes, and give it a month before drawing conclusions. The sites that are improving their INP scores right now are positioning themselves ahead of competitors who haven't figured out why their rankings have been wobbly since March.
