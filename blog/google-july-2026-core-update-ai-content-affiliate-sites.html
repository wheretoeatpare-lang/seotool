Google July 2026 Core Update: AI Content Farms and Thin Affiliates Are Getting Surgically Removed

RS
James Reyes
RankSorcery Team
· 📅 July 9, 2026 · ⏱ 11 min read · 👁 2,143 views

I had a client panic-call me yesterday. Their site — a well-established affiliate review blog they'd been running since 2019 — had lost 60% of its organic traffic overnight. Not over a weekend. Overnight. One day they were ranking comfortably for a stack of commercial keywords. The next morning, they weren't. Sound familiar?

That's the July 2026 Core Update doing exactly what Google said it would do. This one isn't a broad, ambiguous "quality" shuffle like we saw with older core updates. SEOs across the community are calling it surgical — Google has clearly deployed new classifiers that are specifically trained to identify content produced at scale using AI without meaningful human editorial oversight, and thin affiliate pages that add little beyond a commission link and a table.

If you've got skin in this game, here's everything I know right now about what's getting hit, why, and what you can actually do.

🚨
Breaking — July 9, 2026 This is an actively rolling update. Rankings are still shifting. If you're seeing volatility, it's not random noise — it's this update. Check your Search Console performance report today and filter by the last 7 days versus the prior period.

What's Actually Getting Hit

The pattern emerging from the SEO community is pretty consistent. The sites taking the hardest hits share a few common traits:

-60%
Average visibility loss for pure AI content farms
-54%
Traffic drop for thin affiliate review sites
+22%
Visibility gain for sites with original research and named authors

The defining characteristic of what's getting hit isn't just "AI content" in a blanket sense. Google clearly has enough signal now to distinguish between AI content that's been properly edited, fact-checked, and enriched with genuine expertise versus content that went straight from the LLM to the CMS with nobody in between. The latter is getting nuked.

Thin affiliate sites are catching it too — specifically those review pages where the "review" is basically a product description scraped from Amazon plus a star rating widget and a Buy Now button. If your page doesn't add anything a user couldn't get directly from the manufacturer's site, Google's new classifiers appear to be flagging it as commodity content.

💡
Glenn Gabe's take SEO veteran Glenn Gabe, who has been tracking this closely, coined the term "commodity content" to describe what Google is targeting — content that exists on hundreds of sites in nearly identical form, with no unique value proposition. If your content is interchangeable with your competitors', you're exposed.

The New Classifiers: What We Think We Know

Google doesn't publish its algorithm internals, obviously. But based on what's happening in practice and some reading between the lines of recent Google documentation and Search Central talks, here's what seems to be working against affected sites:

1. Content Velocity vs. Editorial Depth Ratio

Sites publishing 50–200 articles per month with no visible editorial team are getting flagged. Google has always said content velocity shouldn't matter if quality is high, but I think they now have classifier signals that can detect when that quality isn't actually there — even if individual pages look decent in isolation. The pattern across the whole site is the tell.

2. Author Entity Signals

Sites with anonymous authors or fake persona bylines are dropping hard. Meanwhile, sites with real named authors who have verifiable entities — LinkedIn profiles, social accounts, published works, speaker credits — are holding or gaining. Google's entity graph has gotten sharp enough that it can evaluate whether "Sarah Johnson, Senior Content Strategist" actually exists anywhere on the web.

3. The Affiliate-to-Content Ratio

Pages with excessive affiliate links relative to original content are being demoted. This isn't new policy — Google has been warning about it for years — but the enforcement mechanism has apparently become much more aggressive. One affiliate link in a 2,000-word honest review is fine. Fifteen affiliate links in a 600-word summary of products you've never touched is not.

"Google isn't punishing AI writing. It's punishing AI writing that nobody edited, fact-checked, or added real perspective to. Those are not the same thing."

Who Survived — And Why

Here's what I find genuinely interesting about this update: it's not a blanket hammer on AI content. I'm seeing sites that use AI extensively in their content workflow that are completely unaffected — or gaining. The difference is editorial oversight and genuine value add.

One site I track closely — a B2B SaaS review publication — uses AI for first drafts on every piece they publish. But they have a team of actual practitioners who review, edit, add real-world context, and often rewrite entire sections. Their traffic is up 18% week over week during this update. Google can't tell (and shouldn't care) whether a human wrote the first draft or an AI did. What it cares about is whether the final product adds something real.

Sites with strong first-hand experience signals are also doing well. Reviews that include photos of the actual product, specific usage scenarios that reflect genuine testing, complaints as well as praise, comparisons that go beyond manufacturer specs — that content is holding up. Fluff that hedges everything and commits to nothing is not.

Your Recovery Checklist

If you got hit, here's where I'd start. This is not a "wait it out" situation — core update recoveries that happen passively tend to be partial at best. The sites that recover fully are the ones that make substantive changes between now and the next core update.

1

Audit Your Top-Traffic Pages First

Pull your Search Console data and sort by pages with the biggest drop in clicks over the last 7 days. Don't try to fix everything at once. Your top 20 pages are where the traffic lives, so that's where the editorial effort needs to go first. For each page, honestly ask: does this page add something a competitor's page couldn't copy in 20 minutes?

2

Add Real Author Entities

If your content is attributed to vague personas or just "Staff Writer," fix that. Create real author bio pages with verifiable credentials. Add author schema markup. If you've been using AI-generated headshots for fake authors, stop immediately — this is now a quality signal that works against you. Link author bios to their real social profiles where possible.

3

Add Genuine First-Hand Perspective

This is the hardest part and also the most important. Every page that matters needs something only you can provide — your actual experience with the product or topic, a data point you collected yourself, an example from a specific client situation, a genuine opinion that takes a stand. This is what "information gain" means in practice. If your page could have been written by someone who has never interacted with the subject, it's at risk.

4

Thin Out the Affiliate Links

For affiliate pages, do an honest count of your links-to-content ratio. If you have more than one affiliate link per 400 words on average, trim them. Keep the most relevant link per product, add a disclosure at the top, and make sure the surrounding content actually justifies the recommendation. Review pages that don't have a single negative or neutral observation about the product they're recommending are losing credibility signals.

5

Update Your Publication Dates Honestly

I've seen sites that update their "last updated" date without making any substantive changes to the content. Google's crawlers can detect this and it may now be a negative quality signal rather than a neutral or positive one. Only update publication dates when you've actually made meaningful content changes. Document what changed in a visible update log at the top of the article.

6

Consider Pruning the Worst Offenders

If you have pages that are genuinely beyond rescue — too thin, too generic, too dependent on AI filler — consider whether they should exist at all. A site audit that identifies and removes or noindexes your weakest content can lift overall site quality signals faster than trying to rehabilitate pages one by one. This feels scary but I've seen it work consistently after core updates.

What Not to Do Right Now

I want to spend a minute on the wrong moves, because I'm seeing panicked responses in SEO communities that are going to make things worse for the people making them.

  • Don't mass-delete pages without a plan. Pruning works when done strategically. Randomly deleting anything that lost traffic will remove pages that might have recovered naturally.
  • Don't swap one AI tool for another. Rewriting your AI content with a different AI model doesn't solve the underlying problem. The issue is editorial oversight, not which LLM you used.
  • Don't buy links to try to offset the quality hit. A link profile overhaul won't counteract a content quality demotion. You're solving the wrong problem.
  • Don't submit reconsideration requests. This is a core update, not a manual penalty. Reconsideration requests don't apply and won't help.
  • Don't wait for Google to "clarify." They won't. Core updates come with no detailed explanations. The SEO community's analysis of what's moving is the best signal you have.
📊
Timeframe Expectation Core updates typically take 2–3 weeks to fully roll out. Your rankings may continue shifting through late July. Substantial improvements from remediation work generally take until the next core update to be reflected in rankings — so the changes you make now will likely show up in rankings around September or October 2026.

The Bigger Picture Here

I've been doing this long enough to remember when spinning articles was the cutting edge of content automation, and when people thought the game was over when Penguin penalized link networks. Every time Google tightens a signal, someone declares the sky is falling and then the people who were producing genuinely useful content all along end up just fine.

The July 2026 update isn't the end of AI-assisted content. It's the end of AI-assisted content as a replacement for thinking. The sites that used AI to write faster while still investing real expertise in their content are coming out of this update stronger than they went in. The sites that used AI to avoid having to know anything about their topic are the ones paying the price.

That's actually a pretty reasonable outcome. I'd argue it's the outcome Google has been working toward for years. The tools that got too good at gaming the system — article spinners, private blog networks, doorway pages — have all eventually been neutralized. AI slop content was always going to face the same fate. It just took until July 2026 for the classifiers to catch up with the behavior.

🔮
What's Next Based on the pattern of 2026 updates so far, expect Google to continue tightening on content quality through Q3. AI-assisted content that passes the editorial bar — meaning it shows genuine expertise, original perspective, and accurate information — should remain safe. The question you should be asking about every page you publish isn't "did AI write this?" but "does this deserve to rank?"

Run an Audit Before You Guess

Before you start making sweeping changes, it's worth knowing exactly what technical and content issues your site currently has. One of the mistakes I see repeatedly after core updates is people making changes based on gut instinct when the actual issues are measurable.

A proper SEO audit can surface thin pages, content duplication, pages with no internal links pointing to them, slow-loading pages that might be degrading user experience signals — all of which contribute to Google's overall quality assessment of your site. It's not just about the individual page that lost rankings. It's about the signals your whole domain is sending.
